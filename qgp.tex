\subsection{Existence of Quark Gluon Plasma}
The study of heavy ion collisions and the quark gluon plasma produced in these collisions at very high energy densities developed out of a desire to understand the thermodynamic evolution of QCD matter and QCD matter in extreme conditions, such as high densities and temperatures.
This goal brought together developing knowledge of QCD with statistical physics, classical and quantum field theory to understand the role that QCD matter played in the early universe~\cite{Busza:2025early_history_qgp}.
 
One early statistical theory informing QGP theoretical development was the Hagedorn bootstrap model, in which an exponentially growing hadronic mass spectrum, $\rho(m) \sim e^{m/T_0}$, leads to a limiting temperature $T_0 \approx 160~\mathrm{MeV}$ beyond which hadronic matter could not be heated further~\cite{Hagedorn1965}. 
With developments in QCD and the discovery of asymptotic freedom, the result was then later interpreted to be a signature of a phase transition to a deconfined state of QCD matter. 

Numerical confirmation of this deconfinement transition came later from Lattice QCD, which is formulated as a non-perturbative framework for studying QCD.
Lattice QCD numerically evaluates the QCD path integral on a discrete Euclidean space-time lattice, enabling first-principles calculations of thermodynamic properties.~\cite{Wilson1974}. 
Early Lattice QCD studies demonstrated both confinement at low temperature and its disappearance above a critical temperature, providing direct evidence for a deconfinement transition \cite{Creutz1980,McLerranSvetitsky1981}.

These results of a phase transition to a deconfined state of matter at a critical temperature of about 150 MeV from Lattice QCD calculations \cite{Aoki:2006we} and the development of the Bjorken energy density relationship \cite{bjorken_energy_density}, discussed in detail in the following section, provided a theoretical framework for understanding the conditions necessary for the formation of a quark gluon plasma (QGP) in heavy ion collisions.
Further developments in the understanding of the QGP and its properties beyond its deconfined nature were revealed once heavy ion experiments were up and running. 
These discoveries include the QGP's nearly perfect fluid behavior and the discovery of energy loss as hard probes traverse the plasma in heavy ion collisions~\cite{PHENIX_white_paper,STAR_white_paper,PHOBOS_white_paper,BRAHMS_white_paper}.

The QGP behaves as an almost perfect fluid with an extremely small shear viscosity to entropy density ratio, $\eta/s$, approaching the KSS quantum lower bound of $\eta/s = 1/4\pi$~\cite{kss_lower_bound}.
This property was first inferred from the large elliptic flow observed at RHIC~\cite{STAR:2000ekf}, which indicated that strong initial state collective behavior and pressure gradients are preserved through the hydrodynamic evolution of the plasma, thereby requiring that the plasma have a very low shear viscosity~\cite{PHENIX:2006iih,Heinz:2024qgp_hydrodynamics_review}. 
These findings unexpectedly established the QGP as a strongly coupled fluid rather than a weakly interacting gas.

A second key discovery is the phenomenon of jet quenching, where high transverse momentum partons produced in initial hard scatterings lose substantial energy while traversing the QGP. 
Experimentally, this is observed as a suppression of high-$p_T$ hadron yields in central nucleus-nucleus collisions relative to proton-proton collisions. 
This energy loss is arises from both collisional and radiative interactions of early-formed particles with the deconfined medium and provides a probe of QCD matter at high temperature and density \cite{PhysRevLett.88.022301,jet_quenching_Cunqueiro:2021wls}. 
Together, these collective flow and jet quenching results provide complementary evidence that the QGP is a strongly coupled and dense state of matter.

\subsection{Relativistic Heavy Ion Collision Phenomenology}
Heavy ion collisions can be described in terms of a sequences of states that the system is in over time, beginning with the initial state of the colliding nuclei and ending with the experimentally detected final state particles.
The system goes through extreme changes in energy density and temperature through this evolution; therefore the system is best modeled through a combination of phenomenological models~\cite{Busza_2018,phenomenology_florkowski_2014}. 
Figure~\ref{fig:heavy_ion_collision_evolution} shows a schematic of the phenomenological models used to describe the heavy ion collision evolution.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/chapter1/hic_phenomenology.png}
    \caption{Schematic of the phenomenological models used to describe the heavy ion collision evolution. The evolution stages include: initial state, thermalization, hydrodynamic evolution, hadronization, and freeze-out. Reproduced from \cite{heavy_ion_collision_evolution}.}
    \label{fig:heavy_ion_collision_evolution}
\end{figure}

The initial state of heavy ion collisions can be described in a variety of ways using phenomenological models including kinetic gas~\cite{kinetic_initial_state_Kurkela:2015qoa} theories, phenomenological string~\cite{hijing,ampt,epos} models, and glasma~\cite{ipglasma_Schenke:2012wb} models.
At a finite time after the initial collision, the system undergoes a thermalization process where the system is assumed to reach local thermal equilibrium.
At this point, the system can be described by a hydrodynamical model where the system is treated as a relativistic fluid~\cite{hydro_Noronha-Hostler:2013gga,music_Schenke:2010nt,music_Gale:2012rq,music_Paquet:2015lta}.
Here, the QGP phase is modeled using the equation of state~\cite{eos_borsanyi2010qcd} and viscosity terms~\cite{viscosity_Bernhard:2019bmu,Dore:2020jye,McLaughlin:2021dph} as the system expands and cools.
In practice, the system is evolved hydrodynamically by evaluating the energy density and velocity fields on hypercube lattices.

Once the hypercubes on the surface of the fluid system reach a low enough energy density or temperature, they are each hadronized locally using the Cooper-Frye method~\cite{cooper_frye_1974} which samples a distribution of particles from these fluid cells assuming a grand canonical ensemble of particle states.
The resulting hadrons are then developed through a particle cascade where elastic and inelastic collisions and particle decays are simulated~\cite{urqmd_Bass:1998ca,urqmd_Bleicher:1999xi,SMASH:2016zqf}.
Finally, the system is frozen out when the expansion time is greater than or equal to the particle collision time and the final state particles are available for analysis and comparison to experimental data~\cite{kinetic_freezeout}.
A suite of event generators are used to model this evolution of the heavy ion collision system, including some or all of these steps, to make comparisons to experimental data.
Descriptions of the set of event generators used for comparison to and extraction of truth level information from experimental data for the analyses in this thesis are included in Sections~\ref{sec:pp_generators} and \ref{sec:auau_generators}.

\subsubsection{Relativistic Heavy Ion Collision Geometry}
Figure~\ref{fig:lightcone_diagram} outlines the progression in time of heavy ion collisions from initial nuclei moving with ultra-relativistic speed along the beam axis, z axis in the diagram, to the final-state particles produced in the collision.
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/chapter1/hic_geometry_lightcone.png}
    \caption{Lightcone diagram for heavy ion collisions. The collision occurs along the z axis (horizontal) and time (vertical). The evolution from QGP phase to hadrons is shown as the system evolves in time (vertically). Reproduced from \cite{rapidity_review}.}
    \label{fig:lightcone_diagram}
\end{figure}
The dynamics of heavy ion collisions are dominated by ultra-relativistic motion along the beam axis.
Therefore, rapidity is a natural variable to describe the kinematics of the system along this axis, as differences in rapidity are invariant under Lorentz boosts. 
Rapidity is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    y = \frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{E + p_z}{E - p_z}\right),
\end{equation}
where $E$ is the particle energy and $p_z$ is the longitudinal momentum.

However, in experimental analyses, pseudorapidity is more commonly used and is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \eta = -\ln\left[\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the polar angle of the particle with respect to the beam axis.
Pseudorapidity can be directly related to detector geometry, as it depends only on the angular position of a detector level object (track, calorimeter tower, jet), and does not require knowledge of the object's energy or mass. 
In the very high-momentum (or massless) limit, $\eta \approx y$, making pseudorapidity a good approximation to rapidity for most particles produced in ultra-relativistic collisions.

\subsection{Initial State and Bjorken Energy Density}
In an idealized picture of heavy ion collisions, the energy density of the system just after the collision, but just prior to the onset of hydrodynamic evolution, can be estimated by measuring the average energy of the final state particles in the central rapidity plateau region. 
In this idealized model proposed by Bjorken \cite{bjorken_energy_density}, the evolution of the QGP system is treated as a boost-invariant, relativistic fluid and final-state effects are assumed to be minimal. 

Under these assumptions, Bjorken estimated the initial energy density that forms in the central rapidity region between two receding Lorentz contracted nuclei.
To do so, he used information about the mean number of charged particles as a function of rapidity for nucleon-nucleon collisions measured in data, the average energy per nucleon from collision data, and the assumption that for a collision system of nuclei, the average energy as a function of rapidity should be additive. 
From these initial estimates, the energy density in the central rapidity region for A+A collisions should be on the order of 1 to 10 GeV/fm$^3$ \cite{bjorken_energy_density}.
Even though this model is highly idealized, its simplicity makes it extremely useful for connecting experimental measurements back to the initial state, and it has remained largely valid as a benchmark even with the availability of much more sophisticated modeling.  

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/chapter1/bjorken_energy_schematic.png}
    \caption{Schematic for initial state Bjorken energy density calculations of plasma produced in heavy ion collisions. Reproduced from \cite{bjorken_energy_density}.}
    \label{fig:bjorken_energy_schematic}
\end{figure}
Figure~\ref{fig:bjorken_energy_schematic} shows a schematic of the geometric picture Bjorken used to develop this initial state energy density relationship defined in Equation~\ref{eq:bjorken_e}.
Using this diagram and with the knowledge that energy density is defined by $\epsilon$ = total energy / total volume, the total energy of the plasma in the central region of of interest is defined by:
\begin{equation}
    E = \int_{-d}^{d} \frac{dE_{T}}{dx} dx
\end{equation}
Assuming that particles are expanding longitudinally and that the particle movement in the transverse plane is negligible with respect to the expansion along the collision axis, the particle rapidity can be given by $y = \tanh ^{-1}(v_{x}) = \tanh ^{-1}(\frac{x}{\tau_{0}})$, where $\tau_{0}$ is the formation time of the plasma.

The $dE_{T}/dx$ term can now be written in terms of rapidity by taking the derivative of $dy/dx$ and evaluating at $x$ = 0 to get a final result for the total energy of the system:
\begin{equation}
    E = \int_{-d}^{d} \frac{dE_{T}}{dy} \frac{dy}{dx} dx = \frac{dE_{T}}{dy} \int_{-d}^{d} \frac{1}{\tau_{0}} dx = \frac{2d}{\tau_{0}} \frac{dE_{T}}{dy}
\end{equation}
The total volume can be defined as just a cylinder with height $2d$ and radius $R$, giving a final relationship for the initial state energy density of: 
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{Bj} = \frac{dE_{T}}{dy} \frac{1}{\tau_{0} \pi R^2}
    \label{eq:bjorken_e}
\end{equation}
Through this model, the mean initial energy densities for a wide variety of collision systems can be inferred from experimental measurements of \detdeta, and/or subsequent measurements of $dE_{T}/dy$.

The energy density in central collisions of \auau ions at \sqsntwo has been measured to be greater than 4 GeV~\cite{STAR_detdeta,PHENIX_detdeta}. 
In comparison, the energy density inside of a typical hadron is about 0.5 GeV \cite{Busza_2018}. 
Therefore, these early \detdeta measurements were integral in demonstrating the presence of a necessary condition for QGP formation in heavy ion collisions with initial state energy densities well above the theoretical predictions from Lattice QCD for the transition to a deconfined phase of nuclear matter~\cite{Aoki:2006we}.

\subsection{Glauber model}

In studies of the full ensemble or total cross section of nuclei-nuclei collisions in heavy ion physics, the initial geometry of the collision is often modeled using the Glauber model, which has its origins in the multiple scattering theories developed by Glauber in the 1950s \cite{glauber_lecture}.
The basic assumption of the Glauber model used for initial state modeling in heavy ion collisions is that at sufficiently high energies, individual nucleon-nucleon collisions within colliding nuclei can be treated as independent and incoherent processes since (1) the nucleons carry sufficient momentum so that they are essentially undeflected when the nuclei pass through each other and (2) the size of the nucleus is large compared to the range of the nucleon-nucleon force.

The Glauber model was originally used in modeling nuclear collisions using the optical limit approximation which treated the colliding nuclei as smooth, continuous matter distributions. 
Both this model and the later developed Monte Carlo based model use two key pieces of information from experiment to model the initial geometry of the collision: the nuclear density distribution and the nucleon-nucleon cross section at a given collision energy.
The nuclear density distribution is typically modeled using a Woods-Saxon distribution given by:
\begin{equation}
    \rho(r) = \rho_0 \frac{1 + w(r/R)^2}{1 + e^{\frac{r-R}{a}}}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_0$ is the density of the nucleus at its center, $R$ is the radius of the nucleus, $a$ is "skin depth" which describes how quickly the density falls off at the edge of the nucleus, and $w$ describes deviations from spherical symmetry. 
For Au+Au collisions, the nominal values for these parameters are $R = 6.38$ fm, $a = 0.535$ fm and $w = 0$.

The nucleon-nucleon cross section has been determined from measurements of proton-proton collisions at a range of collision energies. The world reference for the proton-proton cross section as a function of collision energy is shown in Figure \ref{fig:pp_cross_section}.
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/chapter1/pp_cross_section.png}
    \caption{World reference of p+p cross section as a function of collision energy. Reproduced from \cite{PDG_2012}.}
    \label{fig:pp_cross_section}
\end{figure}

In the optical limit model, the total cross section for a collision of two nuclei is calculated by integrating over the density profiles of the projectile and target nuclei area to determine the probability of a collision.
Figure~\ref{fig:optical_glauber_model} shows a schematic of the optical limit Glauber model where the nuclei are separated in transverse direction by a distance $b$ and a small overlap area between the two nuclei is highlighted in grey.
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/chapter1/optical_glauber.png}
    \caption{Schematic of the optical limit Glauber model. Target A and Projectile B are given by continuous matter distribution and are moving towards one another along the beam axis ($z$ direction). Impact parameter between nuclei is given by $b$. Reproduced from \cite{glauber_review}.}
    \label{fig:optical_glauber_model}
\end{figure}
Following from Figure~\ref{fig:optical_glauber_model}, the probability of a collision per unit area of a nuclei or "thickness function" is given by $ \hat{T}_{A}(\textbf{s}) = \int \hat{\rho}_{A}(\textbf{s},z) dz$, where $\hat{\rho}_{A}$ is the normalized nuclear density distribution of the nucleus A and $\textbf{s}$ is the transverse position in the plane perpendicular to the beam axis. 
Then, the collision overlap function of two nuclei A and B can be calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    T_{AB}(\textbf{b}) = \int \hat{T}_{A}(\textbf{s}) \hat{T}_{B}(\textbf{s}-\textbf{b}) d^2s
\end{equation}
The probability of $n$ nucleon-nucleon collisions between the two nuclei with total A and B nucleons can then be calculated in terms of $T_{AB}$ and the nucleon-nucleon cross section, $\sigma_{NN}$, using the binomial distribution as:
\begin{equation}    
    P_{coll}(n,\textbf{b}) = \binom{AB}{n} [\sigma_{NN} \hat{T}_{AB}(\textbf{b})]^{n} [1 - \sigma_{NN} T_{AB}(\textbf{b})]^{AB-n}
\end{equation}
From this probability distribution, the total cross section of the nucleus-nucleus collision, the number of binary collisions, and the number of participating nucleons can be calculated for a given impact parameter.
For unpolarized symmetric nuclei, the vector impact parameter $\textbf{b}$ can also be simplified to a scalar impact parameter, b, which is just the transverse distance between the centers of the two colliding nuclei.
The total cross section is then calculated by integrating over the impact parameter as:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{A+B} = 2 \pi \int b \sum_{n=1}^{AB} P_{coll}(n,b) db = 2 \pi \int b [1 - (1 - \sigma_{NN} T_{AB}(b))^{AB}] db
\end{equation}
Collisions are mapped between theory and experiment using the number of participant nucleons, \Npart, and number of binary collisions, \Ncoll, since these quantities are accessible both from Glauber model calculations and from experimental measurements using comparisons to the Glauber model.
The details of extracting these quantities from theoretical calculations and simulations are included below while additional details on the extraction of \Npart and \Ncoll from experimental data are included in Section~\ref{sec:detdeta_selection}.
The number of participant nucleons, \Npart, is the number of nucleons that undergo at least one inelastic collision, and the number of binary collisions, \Ncoll, is the total number of inelastic nucleon-nucleon collisions that occur in a given heavy ion collision. 
\Ncoll for a set impact parameter can be calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    N_{coll}(b) = AB \sigma_{NN} T_{AB}(b)
\end{equation}
\Npart for a set impact parameter can be calculated as: 
\begin{equation}
    N_{part}(\textbf{b}) = A \int \hat{T}_{A}(\textbf{s}) [1 - (1 - \sigma_{NN} \hat{T}_{B}(\textbf{s}-\textbf{b}))^{B}] d^2s + B \int \hat{T}_{B}(\textbf{s}-\textbf{b}) [1 - (1 - \sigma_{NN} \hat{T}_{A}(\textbf{s}))^{A}] d^2s
\end{equation}
which can be understood in both terms as the thickness function $\hat{T}$ from one nuclei multiplied by the nucleon-nucleus cross section of the other nuclei. 
The Glauber model in the optical limit can directly use the above equations to determine these collision parameters for a fixed impact parameter.
However, it is important to note that since the optical model describes the nuclei as a set continuous density distribution, there are no event-by-event fluctuations included in the model and values for collision information like \Npart are set for a particular impact parameter.

The Glauber model was later extended to include a Monte Carlo implementation, which allowed for the modeling of nuclei using individually placed nucleons rather than as continuous matter distributions.
In the Monte Carlo Glauber model, the Woods-Saxon distribution is now sampled to determine the positions of individual nucleons within the colliding nuclei on an event-by-event basis allowing for event-by-event fluctuations in the nuclear geometry. 
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/chapter1/mc_glauber.png}
    \caption{Schematic of the Monte Carlo Glauber model with projectile (blue) and target (red) nuclei formed from nucleon sampling. Left: Overlap of collision region between projectile and target. Right: Beam axis view of collision between projectile and target. Participant nucleons given in bright red/blue. Spectator nucleons given in light red/blue. Reproduced from \cite{glauber_review}.}
    \label{fig:mc_glauber_model}
\end{figure}
The result of this sampling procedure is shown in a schematic in Figure \ref{fig:mc_glauber_model}.
Then, the nucleon-nucleon cross section is used to determine if a collision occurs between two nucleons by requiring that the transverse distance between two nucleons be within $\sqrt{\sigma_{NN}/\pi}$, where $\sigma_{NN}$ is the nucleon-nucleon cross section at the given collision energy.
This method allows for the modeling of fluctuations in the initial geometry of the collision and becomes important when comparing to the results from the optical limit model in both very central and very peripheral events.
The MC Glauber method also includes nuclear shadowing effects, in which during a projectile nucleon's interactions with multiple target nucleons, the target nucleons can "hide" behind each other thereby lowering the overall total cross section.
This effect is not included in the optical limit model and a noticeable difference in the optical model calculation of the total cross section and the MC Glauber model calculation is observed compared to the measured total cross section.
The difference extracted in cross section distributions from the optical and MC Glauber models as a function of impact parameter are shown for Au+Au collisions in Figure~\ref{fig:cross_section_vs_b}.

\subsection{\detdeta Measurements}

Measurements of the mean transverse energy and corresponding calculation of the initial state energy density have been studied by many detectors at RHIC and the LHC for both heavy ion collisions (Au+Au and Pb+Pb) and small systems (such as p+p, p+Pb and d+Au) \cite{PHENIX_detdeta,STAR_detdeta,cms_pbpb_detdeta,alice_detdeta,cms_pPb_detdeta,atlas_detdeta}. 
These experiments have for the large part employed two distinct methods of measuring total transverse energy.
The first method has been used by PHENIX, ATLAS and CMS \cite{PHENIX_detdeta,cms_pbpb_detdeta,atlas_detdeta}, where the transverse energy in the detectors' calorimeters are measured and these measurements are corrected for detector effects, limits in detector acceptance and detector hadronic response back to the truth \detdeta of the collisions, which includes both hadronic and electromagnetic energy contributions. 
This method works best in calorimeter systems with a large number of radiation and interaction lengths, allowing for the majority if not all of the collision energy to be captured by the detector. 
This methodology was employed by PHENIX \cite{PHENIX_detdeta} using only an electromagnetic calorimeter with $\lesssim 1$ hadronic interaction lengths; instead PHENIX corrected back to the truth \detdeta from a combination of electromagnetic energy, minimum ionizing particles and a small contribution from hadronic showers. 

The second method has been used by STAR and ALICE \cite{STAR_detdeta,alice_detdeta}, in which the charged hadronic component of the transverse energy is measured using the tracking subsystems and the electromagnetic component of the transverse energy is measured using the calorimeter subsystems. 
This measurement method is useful to employ with very precise tracking systems and particle identification. However, in this method it is very important to ensure that the hadronic energy measured by the tracking subsystem is not double-counted in the calorimeter energy measurement. 
Notably, sPHENIX with the first hadronic calorimeter at RHIC is able to capture both the electromagnetic and hadronic energy contributions in a similar manner to the previously listed LHC detectors. 

\Npart and \Ncoll defined in the context of the Glauber model are used to understand the scaling of various observables in heavy ion collisions, such as particle yields and transverse energy, with the geometry of the collision. 
For example, if an observable scales with the number of participant nucleons, \Npart, it suggests that the observable is primarily influenced by the geometry of the collision and the number of nucleons that are involved in the collision. 
On the other hand, if an observable scales with the number of binary collisions, \Ncoll, it suggests that the observable is primarily influenced by hard scatterings between individual nucleons. 

A parameterization of the \Npart and \Ncoll scaling of various observables including the mean transverse energy as a function of pseudorapidity, \detdeta, has been previously investigated in various collision systems and energies at RHIC and the LHC. 
The scaling of \detdeta in heavy ion collisions with \Npart and \Ncoll has been found to be nonlinear (with saturation as a function of \Npart in more central collisions) but well described by a two component parameterization of the form:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dE_{T}^{AA}}{d\eta} = \frac{dE_{T}^{pp}}{d\eta} [(1-x)\langle \Npart \rangle/2 + x\langle \Ncoll \rangle]
\end{equation}
where $x$ is a free parameter that is fit to the data and has a nominal value of 0.08 for Au+Au collisions at \sqsntwo.

However, through the further study of mean transverse energy over multiple centralities for various collision systems, it has been discovered that the mean transverse energy as a function of pseudorapidity scales linearly with the number of constituent-quark participants, $N_{pq}$, in the collision \cite{PHENIX_npq_scaling_detdeta}. 
A comparison of the nonlinear scaling of $\detdeta/0.5\Npart$ and linear scaling of $\detdeta/0.5N_{pq}$ as a function of \Npart is given in Figure~\ref{fig:detdeta_npq}.
This scaling phenomena with number of quark participants suggests that the majority of the entropy in heavy ion collisions is produced very early in the collision prior to hydrodynamic evolution. 
This agrees with the general understanding of the QGP hydrodynamic evolution, where the nearly perfect fluid nature of the QGP with almost no viscosity produces very little entropy in its flow and evolution \cite{bjorken_energy_density,Busza_2018}.

\subsection{Monte Carlo Generators in Au+Au analysis}
\label{sec:auau_generators}
\subsubsection{\hijing}
\hijing (Heavy Ion Jet Interaction Generator)~\cite{hijing} is a Monte Carlo event generator designed for simulating high-energy heavy-ion and proton-proton collisions and is used in this thesis for simulating a set of minimum bias Au+Au collisions at 200 GeV. 
\hijing uses the Glauber model to describe the geometry of the colliding nuclei and perturbative QCD to model hard scatterings as minijets, where a scale $pTmin$ can be used to determine whether an interaction is considered "hard" or "soft."
Multi-parton interactions, initial-state radiation, and final-state radiation are also included in the modeling through this minijet production.
Soft interactions are modeled using the Dual Parton Model (DPM), where the colliding nucleons are represented as color strings.
Both the hard and soft interactions modeled within \hijing are then hadronized into final-state particles using the Lund string model~\cite{lund_frag_model,lund_frag_model_sjostrand}.
Notably, while \hijing models both the initial stage hard and soft interactions and the hadronization stage of heavy ion collisions, \hijing does not include a hydrodynamic evolution stage or allow for coalescence within its hadronization stage, and thus does not fully model the known processes of the QGP phase of heavy ion collision.
To account for this, collective flow effects are added to the final state particle distribution in \hijing via an afterburner within the sPHENIX \geant simulation framework.

\subsubsection{\ampt}
\ampt (A Multi-Phase Transport Model)~\cite{ampt} is a Monte Carlo event generator that simulates heavy-ion collisions by modeling multiple stages of the collision process, including the initial parton production, parton transport, hadronization, and hadronic interactions.
\ampt has a similar theoretical basis to \hijing and uses \hijing to generate the initial parton distribution; these partons are then developed through a parton cascade of 2-to-2 elastic collisions, where the strength of parton interactions determines the transport properties of the QGP.
Hadronization is then modeled using a quark coalescence model. 
Finally, the resulting hadron distribution is developed through a hadron transport model that includes elastic and inelastic scatterings, and resonance decays, to model interactions before the system's kinetic freeze out.
\subsubsection{\epos}
\epos (Energy conserving quantum mechanical multiple scattering approach, based on Partons (parton ladders), Off-shell remnants, Splitting of parton ladders)~\cite{epos} uses an entirely different approach to modeling the initial stage of heavy ion collisions than \hijing and \ampt.
\epos models collisions using an S-matrix approach to Gribov-Regge Theory which simultaneously describes soft and hard scatterings. 
Here \epos allows for multiple scatterings to occur in parallel while accounting for energy conservation for these multiple scatterings.
Instead of using factorization to separate the hard and soft scattering components and sampling from a set PDF, the partons of projectile and target nuclei are evolved using the DGLAP formalism to determine a set of parallel scatterings that occur in the collision.
A schematic of the \epos approach to modeling multiple scatterings is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:epos_smatrix}.
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/chapter1/epos_schematic.png}
    \caption{Schematic of \epos parallel scattering for $n = 3$ parallel scatterings between two nucleons with energy conservation enforced across all exchanges simultaneously. Each of the three Pomeron ladders includes a Born kernel and a radiative component $E$. Reproduced from \cite{epos}.}
    \label{fig:epos_smatrix}
\end{figure}

After the primary interactions are developed based on this S-matrix approach for parallel scatterings, the system undergoes a core-corona separation procedure where the core (high-density region) undergoes hydrodynamic evolution then hadronization and the corona (low-density region) is treated as independent strings and hadronized using the Lund string model.
In the core, initial conditions of the hydrodynamic modeling, including energy density, velocity, conserved charges, are determined from the dense parton strings and the system is evolved using 3+1D hydrodynamics with a realistic equation of state and viscosity terms.
Finally, these hydrodynamic fluid cells are hadronized using the Cooper-Frye method and the resulting hadrons are then developed through a hadronic cascade where, similar to \ampt, elastic and inelastic collisions and particle decays are simulated.
