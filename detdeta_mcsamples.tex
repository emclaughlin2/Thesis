The \detdeta measurement, summing over final particles of all energies, is sensitive to all aspects of the collision event, from the initial state through hadronization and final state interactions. 
Therefore, multiple events generators with different underlying physics modeling are used for comparison in this \detdeta measurement allowing for insights into how different heavy ion collision phenomena impact \detdeta. 
Minimum bias \auau collision events at \sqsntwo were produced from three different MC event generators: \hijing~\cite{hijing}, \ampt~\cite{ampt} and \epos~\cite{epos}.
For all three generators, the events were then passed through a full \geant~\cite{GEANT4} simulation of the sPHENIX detector and reconstruction chain to produce detector-level objects comparable to those in data. \textcolor{red}{There is some repetition between what is here and what appears at the end of section 4.1.}
A total of ~1M events for each MC generator were used in this analysis.
For determining the MC-based correction factors used for mapping the detector-level \detdeta to the truth particle level \detdeta, the MC events are weighted to match the $z$-vertex and particle composition in data to limit model dependence in the final measurements.

\subsubsection{Determining MC Particle Composition Reweighting Factors}

Reweight factors were created for final state particles in each of the three MC generators.
Using both the final state particle spectra of the three MC generators and the measured identified particle spectra from PHENIX and STAR, the invariant yields of $\pi^{\pm}$, $K^{\pm}$, $p$, $\bar{p}$, $\Lambda$, and $\bar{\Lambda}$ as functions of transverse momentum ($p_{T}$) were analyzed across various centrality bins. 
The Monte Carlo particle spectrum was calculated for models like \hijing, \epos and \ampt, utilizing identical centrality binning and $\eta$ acceptance range as was used in the PHENIX and STAR measurements. 
The MC generator spectra of $\pi^{\pm}$, $K^{\pm}$, $p$, and $\bar{p}$ were then compared to PHENIX data, while $\Lambda$ and $\bar{\Lambda}$ were compared to STAR data to compute the data/MC ratios. 

PHENIX data was specifically chosen for the p, K and $\pi$ particle reweighting procedure as it was feed-down corrected for contributions from weak decays, while the STAR data included these contributions. 
As an additional check, a comparison between the previously measured $\pi$ invariant yield in STAR and PHENIX data for three separate centrality ranges was performed as $\pi$ yields should still be affected by the feed-down corrections, but less so then protons or kaons.
Since the STAR data does not include feed-down corrections it was expected that this measurement of invariant yield of pions to be slightly higher in the lower $p_{T}$ range than the pion yield from PHENIX. 
In the comparison of STAR and PHENIX $\pi$ yield in Figure~\ref{fig:star_phenix_comparison}, small differences were observed in the $\pi$ particle spectra at low $p_{T}$ ($\sim 1$ GeV) due to the different feed-down corrections; otherwise, the spectra showed good agreement.

\subsubsection{Applying MC Particle Composition Reweighting Factors}

The reweighting factors determined from the data/MC ratios of identified particle spectra are propagated to the detector-level calorimeter towers and final state truth particles of the MC generator simulation events after running through the full \geant detector simulation.
This is afterburner style reweighting is accomplished for the detector-level calorimeter towers by first tracing back each G4Hit for each calorimeter tower in the detector to its originating final state particle. 
The reweighting factor for each hit is then calculated based on the primary parent particle's particle ID (PID), transverse momentum ($p_{T}$), and the event's centrality. 
This factor is derived from interpolating the MC/data ratios across different centrality bins as a function of $p_{T}$ and using fits like those shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hijing_data_mc_ratios} to ensure a precise adjustment of the G4Hit energy.
This same reweighting factor is then also applied to update the final state particles' four-momenta in the truth record for the truth $\ET$ calculation.

Although this approach does not replicate the precise conditions of each individual event, as this approach equates one 2 GeV $\pi$ with two 1 GeV $\pi$, it effectively incorporates the average energy contributions from the data into the MC simulations across a multitude of events. \textcolor{red}{I don’t understand this statement.}
The motivation for this reweighting method was to align the average energy contributions from simulated data more closely with the actual experimental observations. 
By applying these reweighting factors, the method on average adjusts the MC simulation to approach the empirical data, thereby ensuring that the particle average \detdeta in the simulation is representative of real-world observations.
